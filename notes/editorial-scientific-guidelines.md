# Editorial and scientific guidelines

These conventions govern the public-facing scientific text of the project. They are intentionally compact: local chapter-specific decisions should remain in the chapter unless they generalize to the eBook as a whole.

1. **Write for a specialized but cross-disciplinary external reader.** Each chapter must be self-contained enough that a specialist in one project domain can follow another domain without recent prior study. Do not mention internal milestones, gates, planning discussions, development instructions, repository workflow, editorial contracts, intended-audience strategy or the pedagogical method itself in the public narrative; the text should embody those choices rather than describe them.
2. **Prefer the pedagogical sequence intuition → definition → example → application when it improves comprehension.** Introduce the physical, geometric or computational idea before formal notation, define it rigorously, provide a minimal example when useful and connect it to the aero-stealth problem. Do not add examples merely for length, and do not repeat this editorial sequence explicitly in the chapter prose.
3. **Introduce concepts before specialized variants.** Umbrella concepts imported from another discipline must be defined before they are used to classify methods or architectures. In particular, terms such as Message Passing Neural Network (MPNN), Geometric Deep Learning (GDL), equivariance, neural operator, multifidelity learning, uncertainty quantification and active learning require a concise definition and a statement of how they relate to neighboring concepts used in the book.
4. **Introduce context and symbols before using them.** A new equation must not appear before the reader knows the meaning of its variables, operators and relevant assumptions. Every newly introduced symbol, index, set, function, superscript, subscript, hat, tilde or parameter in a displayed equation must be defined in the immediately surrounding text. Symbols already defined and used consistently may be referenced rather than redefined. When a concept from another discipline is used only as an example, provide the minimum context required to make the example complete.
5. **Make mathematical mappings intelligible as engineering models.** For operators such as \(\mathcal G:(G,\mathbf c)\mapsto\mathbf y\), define the domain objects, conditioning variables, trainable parameters, predicted quantities and reference quantities before discussing architecture or loss functions. Distinguish clearly between exact/reference quantities and predictions, for example through consistent use of hats for predicted outputs.
6. **State assumptions, units, conventions and domains of validity explicitly when they affect interpretation.** This includes coordinate systems, signs, reference quantities, metric signatures, temporal conventions, constitutive laws, boundary conditions, dimensional versus nondimensional forms and the physical regime in which an approximation is valid.
7. **Use formal, affirmative academic prose.** Prefer third-person scientific writing. Distinguish established results, modeling assumptions, hypotheses to be tested, engineering choices and empirical findings. Avoid presenting planned comparisons or architectural choices as conclusions before evidence is available.
8. **Define acronyms and specialized terminology at first occurrence.** Examples include UAV, UCAV, RCS, CEM, CFD, FDTD, MoM, FEM, PO, RANS, GNN, MPNN, GDL, MDO, NLP, MINLP, UQ and STA. Retain established English technical terms when translation would reduce precision, while explaining them in Portuguese on first use.
9. **Use a consistent international author–year citation convention throughout the book.** Citations must be attached directly to the claim they support. Do not use a raw citation key as the grammatical subject of a sentence (for example, do not write `@key propõe ...`). Prefer parenthetical citations such as `[@key]` after the supported statement. When narrative attribution is scientifically useful, write the author name in normal prose and use author-suppressed Pandoc syntax for the year, for example `Sen [-@sen2023]`, which renders as “Sen (2023)” under an author–year style. Multiple sources supporting one claim should be grouped in a single citation where appropriate.
10. **Use sources according to their evidentiary role.** Prefer peer-reviewed, DOI-verifiable and recent literature for current technical claims, while retaining seminal books and papers for foundations. Do not generalize beyond what a cited source establishes, and do not treat absence in a limited literature search as universal nonexistence.
11. **Preserve the mathematical type of every object.** A vector, bivector, multivector, tensor, linear map, matrix and scalar are not interchangeable merely because they may have coordinate representations of similar size. In particular, a general tensor is not automatically a multivector, and \(u\otimes u\) must not be replaced by \(u^2\).
12. **Use Geometric Algebra as a committed methodological pillar without overstating its consequences.** AG is adopted for geometric and physical representation and for algebraic structures used by learning and optimization. Use multivectors, rotors, duality, outermorphisms and extensors where mathematically appropriate, while retaining conventional tensors, matrices and scalar projections where required.
13. **Maintain rigor when discussing linearity, compactness and computational consequence.** Maxwell equations in linear media are already linear in the fields; STA can unify their representation but should not be described as creating that linearity. Euler equations remain intrinsically nonlinear. Compact notation, coordinate independence, equivariance or a linear representation of an operator must not be equated with lower computational complexity without benchmark evidence.
14. **Distinguish geometric representation from geometric learning.** Geometric Deep Learning is an architectural framework concerned with structures and transformation laws of data; Geometric Algebra is a mathematical formalism. Supplying AG-derived features to a conventional GNN does not by itself make the network equivariant, and storing multivector components numerically does not guarantee that subsequent layers respect their transformation laws.
15. **Describe computational burden through measurable resources.** Prefer processing time, CPU/GPU hours, peak memory, storage, mesh size, number of degrees of freedom, solver evaluations, batch throughput and data-generation/training time. Avoid vague translations such as “cheap/expensive” when the intended meaning is computational demand.
16. **Define fidelity before multifidelity.** Fidelity is the degree of physical and numerical detail with which a model represents a response in a specified regime. A fidelity level should be characterized by assumptions, discretization, constitutive or turbulence models, boundary treatment, convergence criteria and observed resource demand—not only by a solver name or a universal low/high label.
17. **Treat GNN architecture claims as hypotheses until tested.** Shared encoders, specialized heads, multitask learning, long-range mechanisms, GA-informed features, multivector channels, equivariant layers and physics-informed losses may be motivated by structure, but superiority must be established empirically. Define the baseline precisely; terms such as “conventional MPNN” must state what is conventional in the comparison. Physics residuals are valid only when the network outputs contain the variables required to compute those residuals consistently.
18. **Separate surrogate accuracy from engineering usefulness.** Evaluation should include field and integrated-response error where applicable, robustness across geometry, operating conditions and remeshing, computational-resource use, number of reference-solver calls, end-to-end processing time and the quality of optimization solutions after re-evaluation with reference models.
19. **Prevent data leakage when claiming geometric generalization.** If multiple operating conditions exist for the same geometry, geometry-level splitting should be used when the claim concerns unseen configurations. Distinguish interpolation in operating conditions, generalization to unseen geometries, joint geometry/condition extrapolation and sensitivity to remeshing.
20. **Treat deterministic mathematical optimization as a research axis within MDO.** Use constrained nonlinear programming (NLP) for continuous design spaces and mixed-integer nonlinear programming (MINLP) only when genuine discrete or integer decisions are present. Evolutionary or heuristic methods may be comparators rather than the default optimization paradigm.
21. **Treat uncertainty quantification and active learning as optional supporting strategies unless evidence justifies adoption.** Define uncertainty before using it operationally. Active learning should be motivated by the value of selecting additional resource-intensive simulations more efficiently than a fixed design, not included by default.
22. **Validate progressively and re-evaluate final engineering conclusions.** Move from canonical cases to increasingly complex configurations, checking local fields, conservation or boundary consistency and integrated responses as appropriate. Surrogate-predicted optima and Pareto sets must be selectively re-evaluated with reference models; experimental comparison should be used where feasible and relevant.
23. **Keep the research contribution affirmative and precise.** Describe the pursued integration directly: UCAV aero-stealth MDO, mesh-native GNNs, explicit multifidelity learning, angular/polarized RCS, deterministic mathematical optimization, Geometric Algebra, resource accounting and reference-model re-evaluation. Avoid broad novelty claims that are not supported by a documented literature review.
